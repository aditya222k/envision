    What to notice

    • The mass number never changes from world to world. Weight is the pull of gravity on that mass, so weight changes.
    • Same push from the legs, very different heights. Stronger gravity slows you down and pulls you back faster.
    • Watch the time in the air. On the Moon and Pluto the jump plays out in slow motion.
    • Saturn is huge, yet its gravity is close to Earth's. Size alone does not decide gravity; mass and radius both matter.

    Ask the class

    • Before jumping on Jupiter: higher or lower than Earth? By how much?
    • Why does the bathroom scale give a different number when nobody's mass changed?
    • On which world would a basketball game be the most fun? The hardest?

    What to notice

    • Turn off Short click puts it in orbit and click once. With no sideways push, the planet falls straight into the star.
    • Throw too slowly and it crashes. Too fast and it escapes. In between, it orbits.
    • Closer planets move faster and finish their laps sooner.
    • A moon only stays with its planet inside the moon zone (dashed ring). Farther out, the star's pull wins and steals it.
    • Turn off Hold stars in place. The star wobbles too, because planets pull on stars as well.

    Ask the class

    • Why doesn't the Moon fall into Earth if gravity is always pulling it?
    • What would happen to Earth if the Sun's gravity suddenly switched off?
    • Who can build a system with two planets and a moon that survives 30 seconds?

    Gravity Lab

    Drop balls of any size. Pin some in place, let others move, and watch the pulls between them. Bigger balls have more mass and pull harder.

    Big idea: gravity grows with mass and weakens with distance. Equal pulls in opposite directions cancel out.

    Patterns on balls and trails, labelled arrows and clearer outlines, so nothing depends on colour alone.

      What to notice

      • Between two equal fixed balls, the small ball sits still. The two pulls are the same size and point in opposite directions, so they cancel.
      • Press Nudge left. Now the left ball is closer, its pull wins, and the small ball slides toward it.
      • With a big and a small fixed ball, the balance point moves toward the smaller ball. You have to be closer to a small mass to feel an equal pull.
      • In Two balls pull together, both balls feel the same size pull, but the lighter ball speeds up more.

      Ask the class

      • Where is the balance point between Earth and the Moon? (About 90% of the way to the Moon.)
      • If you double the distance between two balls, what happens to the pull? Use the numbers to check.
      • Can you place three fixed balls so a small ball floats in balance between all of them?

        What to notice

        • The engine only fires at launch. After that the rocket coasts, and gravity slows it the whole way up. Watch the speed readout drop.
        • The rocket's own mass is not in the formula. A pebble and a spaceship need the same escape velocity.
        • More mass M means a higher escape velocity. A bigger radius R means a lower one, because you start farther from the center.
        • As the rocket climbs, the planet shrinks behind it and its gravity fades. At twice the distance from the center, gravity is only a quarter as strong.
        • Answers within 1.5% of the true value count as a perfect escape.
        • This game gives the rocket all its speed in one launch boost and ignores air resistance. Real rockets build up speed over several minutes, but the energy needed to escape is the same.

        Ask the class

        • Why doesn't a heavier rocket need a bigger launch speed?
        • Which world is easiest to escape from? Which is hardest? Predict before you calculate.
        • Real rockets keep their engines burning instead of leaving the ground at 11 km/s. Why might that be? (Think about air.)
